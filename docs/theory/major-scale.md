# The major scale

The major scale is the reference point for almost everything else in Western harmony.
Intervals are named relative to it, chords are built from it, and the other common scales
are described as changes to it. Learn this one well and the rest get much cheaper.

## The formula

A major scale is seven notes built from a fixed pattern of whole steps (W, two frets) and
half steps (H, one fret):

```
W  W  H  W  W  W  H
```

Start on any note, apply the pattern, and you land back on the starting note an octave
higher. Starting on C:

| Degree | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 (1) |
|--------|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|-------|
| Note   | C | D | E | F | G | A | B | C     |
| Step   |   | W | W | H | W | W | W | H     |

C major has no sharps or flats, which is why it is the usual teaching example. The pattern
is what matters, not the letter names. G major follows the same steps and gets an F♯;
F major gets a B♭.

## Scale degrees

Each note has a number, its **degree**, counted from the root. Degrees are how musicians
talk about a scale without committing to a key. "The 3rd" means the same thing in every
major scale.

Two degrees do most of the work:

- **The 3rd** decides whether a scale or chord sounds major or minor. In the major scale
  it sits two whole steps above the root (a major third).
- **The 7th** sits one half step below the root and pulls strongly back toward it. That
  pull is a big part of why the major scale feels resolved when you land on 1.

The two half steps in the pattern fall between degrees 3 and 4 and between 7 and 8. Those
are the only places adjacent scale notes are a single fret apart on the same string.

string and climbing two octaves to the C at the 8th fret of the high e string. The roots

Play it slowly, say the degree numbers out loud (1 2 3 4 5 6 7 1), and listen for where
the half steps are: 3 to 4 and 7 to 1 are the only places two scale notes sit one fret
apart on the same string. The other positions, and how they connect, will live in the
[Fretboard](../fretboard/index.md) section.

## Why it matters

- **Intervals** are measured against it. A major third is the distance from 1 to 3; a
  perfect fifth is 1 to 5.
- **Diatonic chords** come from stacking scale notes. Degrees 1, 3, and 5 give the tonic
  triad (C, E, G in C major).
- **Modes** are the same seven notes started from a different degree.
- **The minor scale** is the major scale with the 3rd, 6th, and 7th lowered a half step.

Each of those gets its own page. This one is the foundation they refer back to.
